You do not need a finished vision before speaking with an interior designer. The consultation is a place to explore possibilities, identify what matters, and understand what kind of support your project needs. A few notes and references can make that conversation more focused.

## 1. Begin with the everyday

Think about the moments your home needs to support. Where do you have coffee? How often do friends stay? Does the dining table double as a workspace? Write down what works now and what gets in the way.

Be specific. “We need somewhere comfortable to read after dinner” gives a designer more to work with than “we want a better living room.” Include the needs of everyone who uses the space, and separate daily frustrations from occasional inconveniences.

## 2. Gather a few images—and explain why

A small selection of references is more useful than a large folder of unrelated rooms. For each image, note what draws you in: the light, a color combination, a furniture arrangement, or simply the feeling of calm.

Include things you already own and want to keep. A painting, a family dining table, or a favorite chair may provide a more personal starting point than a trend. You can also browse our [residential projects](/work/) to find examples of the atmosphere you have in mind.

## 3. Bring the practical details

If you have them, gather floor plans, photographs of the existing space, and information about the property. For a renovation, note any work already planned and any professionals already involved.

A condominium may have requirements around work hours, deliveries, and approvals. A second home may need coordination while you are away. These details help define the [design services](/services/) that will be useful to you. Do not worry if you have not collected every document; simply identify what is available.

## 4. Think about your investment and priorities

An initial investment range helps connect the scope with realistic choices. Consider the different parts of a project: design services, construction, finishes, furniture, delivery, and installation. The mix depends on what you are planning.

Make a short list of essentials and a second list of wishes. If your priorities are a more functional kitchen and comfortable seating, say so. That makes it easier to discuss where to focus and whether some work can happen later.

## 5. Share your timing and your questions

Distinguish a firm date, such as a move, from an ideal completion date. Mention anything that could affect access to the property or your availability for decisions. Your designer can then explain what needs to happen before a schedule can be defined.

Ask how decisions are presented, what the proposal includes, and how communication works. Our [design process](/work-process/) offers an overview from consultation through installation, so you can arrive with questions about the stages that matter most to you.

The aim is a shared understanding, not a perfect brief. Bring your questions, your daily routines, and an open mind. The design grows from there.
